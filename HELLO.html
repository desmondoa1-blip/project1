01

One coast, many interiors

Ghana is organised into sixteen regions, each with its own dominant language, landscape and history. A few of the ones travellers and Ghanaians alike talk about most:

Greater Accra

The capital region — Atlantic beaches, the Ga fishing communities, and the political and commercial centre of the country.

Ashanti

Home of the historic Ashanti Kingdom and its capital Kumasi, still the seat of the Asantehene and the Golden Stool.

Central

Cape Coast and Elmina, where centuries-old coastal forts stand as UNESCO World Heritage sites and sites of memory.

Volta

Hilly, lake-bound territory along the Togo border, largely Ewe-speaking, known for waterfalls and Kente weaving towns.

Northern

Savanna country centred on Tamale, with its own chieftaincy traditions distinct from the forest south.

Western

Rainforest and rubber, cocoa and offshore oil — Ghana's timber and energy frontier along the coast to Côte d'Ivoire.

Eastern

Akan heartland threaded by the Akwapim ridge, and home to Lake Volta, one of the largest man-made lakes on earth.

Upper East & West

Ghana's Sahel edge — mud-built architecture, Sirigu wall painting, and a dry-season landscape unlike anywhere else in the country.

02

How the republic was made

Ghana's modern story runs from a gold and trade frontier called the "Gold Coast" by Europeans, through the rise and resistance of the Ashanti Kingdom, to independence and everything after.

Before the 1400s

Akan, Ga, Ewe and other peoples build kingdoms and trading states across the forest and savanna, with gold and kola nut moving along trans-Saharan routes long before Europeans arrive by sea.

1400s–1800s

Portuguese, Dutch, British and other traders build coastal forts and castles to trade gold — and, from the 1500s, enslaved people. The Ashanti Kingdom rises inland as the region's dominant power, ruled through the Golden Stool.

1874–1957

Britain formalises the "Gold Coast" as a colony after decades of Anglo-Ashanti wars. A growing independence movement, led by Kwame Nkrumah and others, presses for self-rule through the 1940s and 50s.

6 March 1957

The Gold Coast becomes Ghana — the first country in Sub-Saharan Africa to gain independence from colonial rule, taking its new name from the medieval Ghana Empire further north and west.

1960 onward

Ghana becomes a republic in 1960, moves through periods of military and civilian rule, and settles into multiparty democracy from 1992, with regular elections and peaceful transfers of power since.

Today

Ghana is a stable multiparty republic under President John Mahama, and one of the more consistently peaceful democracies in the region.

03

Cloth, symbol, and sound

Ghanaian culture carries its own visual and musical language — much of it still made and worn every day, not kept behind glass.

Kente

Woven by Akan and Ewe weavers on narrow looms, Kente cloth is built from strips of geometric pattern stitched together — each pattern and colour combination carries its own name and meaning, worn for festivals, funerals, and formal occasions.

Adinkra symbols

Originally stamped onto funeral cloth by Akan communities, these symbols now appear everywhere from architecture to jewellery — each one a compressed proverb.

Sankofa
go back and reclaim it
Dwennimmen
strength with humility
Gye Nyame
except for God

Music

Highlife — guitar and horn-driven dance music born in the early 20th century — is Ghana's signature export, still audible under hiplife, azonto and the Afrobeats acts that followed it.

Chieftaincy & festival

Traditional chiefs still hold real civic weight alongside elected government. Festivals like Homowo (Ga), Akwasidae (Ashanti) and Hogbetsotso (Ewe) mark the ceremonial calendar with drumming, durbars, and remembrance.

04

What's on the plate

Ghanaian cooking runs on starch, pepper, and slow-built stock — built to be eaten by hand, shared, and argued about (everyone has an opinion on whose jollof is best).

Jollof rice

Rice slow-cooked in a tomato-pepper base until every grain takes on colour and smoke — the centrepiece of most Ghanaian celebrations, and one side of a long-running rivalry with Nigerian jollof.

Waakye

Rice and beans cooked together with dried millet leaves for their deep red-brown colour, served street-side with gari, spaghetti, boiled egg, and a fiery shito pepper sauce.

Banku & tilapia

Fermented corn and cassava dough, pounded smooth, eaten by hand alongside grilled tilapia and a hot pepper-and-onion sauce.

Fufu & light soup

Pounded cassava and plantain, dense and stretchy, torn off and dipped into a peppery, tomato-based soup with goat, chicken, or fish.

Red red & kelewele

Black-eyed peas stewed in red palm oil, usually served beside fried ripe plantain — kelewele's version comes seasoned with ginger, pepper, and cloves.

Akwaaba.

It's the first word most visitors learn, and the one Ghanaians use most — a welcome that shows up on shop signs, church banners, and doorframes across the country, extended long before it's asked for.
